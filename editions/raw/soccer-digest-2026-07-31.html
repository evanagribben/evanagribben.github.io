THE LEAD

Franco Baresi, 1960–2026: the last of the liberi, and the end of Milan's living memory

Milan's eternal captain died on Friday, aged 66, after a long illness. He gave one club twenty seasons, invented a way of defending that the modern game has quietly abandoned, and carried the most famous missed penalty in Italian football without ever once hiding from it. His death lands in the middle of a transfer window that suddenly looks very small.

Franco Baresi died overnight on Thursday into Friday in Milan, aged 66, after an illness of more than a year. AC Milan confirmed the death of the man they had spent the previous forty-eight hours asking the world to leave in peace — the club had issued a statement on 30 July pleading for privacy during what it called "delicate moments," and had publicly denied a premature report of his death that had begun circulating. When the news came for real, the club said almost nothing, which was the correct instinct: "Il nostro numero 6, per sempre. Ciao Capitano." Our number six, forever. Goodbye, captain. The funeral is set for Tuesday 4 August at 11:00 at the Basilica di Sant'Ambrogio, in the city he never left.

The tributes arrived in the particular register Italian football reserves for its own. Paolo Maldini, who inherited the armband and the responsibility, wrote: "Mi hai insegnato a lottare fino all'ultimo respiro" — you taught me to fight until the last breath. Carlo Ancelotti, who played in front of him, and Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni both paid tribute. Gianluigi Buffon, David Beckham and Francesco Totti followed. And on Friday afternoon, in a Hong Kong press room ahead of a pre-season friendly that suddenly felt beside the point, Inter coach Cristian Chivu opened his remarks by crossing the oldest line in the city: "Ci uniamo al mondo dello sport nelle condoglianze alla famiglia di Franco Baresi. Tutti quelli che amano il calcio hanno perso una grande icona di questo sport." Everyone who loves football has lost a great icon of the sport. That it came from the Inter bench is the point. Baresi was rejected by Inter as a boy — they took his brother Giuseppe and passed on Franco as too slight. It remains the most consequential scouting error in the history of the city.

What he became is harder to explain to anyone who did not watch it, because the position no longer exists. Baresi was a libero, a sweeper, and in Arrigo Sacchi's Milan of the late 1980s he was the mechanism by which an entire theory of football was made to work. Sacchi's side pressed high and played a flat back four squeezed thirty yards up the pitch, holding an offside line that no team had previously dared hold. That line does not function without one player empowered to step, to read, and to be right — and Baresi was right with a frequency that still looks like cheating. He was 5ft 9in in an era of giants and he won almost everything in the air anyway, because he arrived before the ball did. Alongside Maldini, Alessandro Costacurta and Mauro Tassotti, he formed a back four that conceded twelve goals in a thirty-four-game Serie A season. Modern coaches talk about the high line as a tactical choice. Baresi made it a personality.

"You taught me to fight until the last breath."

Paolo Maldini, on his captain

The numbers are the least interesting thing about him and they are still extraordinary. Twenty seasons, one club, from his debut as a seventeen-year-old in 1977 to his retirement in 1997. Milan's record books credit him with 719 competitive appearances, 531 of them in Serie A. Six Scudetti. Three European Cups — 1989, 1990 and 1994 — the last of them the 4–0 evisceration of Johan Cruyff's Barcelona in Athens, a night on which Baresi and Costacurta were both suspended and Milan won anyway, which somehow made his authority seem larger rather than smaller. Eighty-one caps for Italy. And, uniquely among great one-club men, he stayed through relegation: when Milan went down to Serie B in 1980 and again in 1982, amid the Totonero betting scandal, the captain went with them. Players of his stature did not do that then and do not do it now.

And then there is the Rose Bowl. On 17 July 1994, Baresi played 120 minutes of a World Cup final twenty-five days after knee surgery, in Pasadena heat, and then missed the first penalty of the shootout. Roberto Baggio missed the last. Brazil won 3–2. The photograph of Baresi doubled over on the halfway line, hands on knees, is one of the images that defines Italian football's relationship with grief. He never once dodged it. He described it, repeatedly and without self-pity, as the worst moment of his career, and then carried on being the most admired defender of his generation. There is something almost unbearably neat about the timing: the World Cup returned to the United States this summer for the first time since that afternoon, finished twelve days ago at MetLife Stadium, and Baresi died before the confetti had been swept up.

His death also lands at an awkward, revealing moment for the club he defined. Milan are on a pre-season tour in Perth under a new coach, Rúben Amorim, signed to 2029. They have just made Gonçalo Ramos the most expensive signing in their history at a reported €65m plus €5m in bonuses from Paris Saint-Germain, surpassing the €49.5m they paid for Rafael Leão — and they are simultaneously trying to sell Leão to Fenerbahçe or Galatasaray at a price that has already fallen from €70m to €50m. On Friday, the Milanese papers carried Baresi's obituary and the Leão haggling on the same pages. The juxtaposition is not a criticism of the modern club so much as a measurement of distance. Baresi played 719 games for one badge for one wage in one city. The club's current defining question is whether a forward will accept €8m a year in Istanbul or hold out for €12m.

The wider point is that the position he perfected has been legislated and coached out of existence. The libero required a defender permitted to be the smartest player on the pitch and trusted to act alone; the modern game asks centre-backs to be ball-progressors first, and protects them with a defensive midfielder, a VAR offside line drawn to the millimetre, and a coaching culture that treats individual improvisation as risk. There are magnificent defenders playing today. There is no one doing Baresi's job, because the job was abolished. That is why his death feels less like the loss of a great player than the closing of a file.

Serie A begins on 22 August. Milan open away at Torino on the 23rd. There will be a minute's silence, and a black armband, and the number six will be somewhere on the shirt. He would, by every account of him, have found the fuss embarrassing and stood through it with the same expression he wore for twenty years: entirely still, watching the whole pitch, already three passes ahead.

The Restoration: Zidane, Mancini and Klopp all took national jobs in eighteen days

Three of the most decorated names in the sport are now international managers, appointed within a fortnight of each other. Two of them have been drawn into the same Nations League group and meet in Paris on 2 October.

On Tuesday 28 July, within hours of each other, the French Football Federation and the FIGC confirmed two of the biggest appointments either country has made this century. Zinédine Zidane is the new manager of France, on a four-year deal running through the 2030 World Cup, taking office on 1 August as the eighteenth man to hold the job since 1964. He succeeds Didier Deschamps, his teammate in the 1998 World Cup-winning side, whose fourteen-year tenure ended with a semi-final defeat to Spain and a bewildering 6–4 loss to England in the third-place play-off. Zidane, 54, has never hidden his intent: "There is nothing greater than the French national team," he said, adding that he had turned down club offers precisely to remain available. FFF president Philippe Diallo called it "an immense source of pride." His backroom staff will be named in early September.

The Italian appointment is a restoration in the literal sense. Roberto Mancini, 61, is Italy's head coach for a second time, on a four-year contract, announced at a federal council meeting by new FIGC president Giovanni Malagò and presented the following day. He returns to a national team that did not play at the World Cup at all — eliminated in the European play-off final in Bosnia and Herzegovina on 31 March, 1–1 after extra time and beaten on penalties, a third consecutive World Cup missed. That defeat detonated the federation: president Gabriele Gravina resigned on 2 April, Gianluigi Buffon stepped down as delegation head, and Gennaro Gattuso's contract was terminated by mutual consent on 3 April. Malagò said Mancini had apologised for the manner of his 2023 departure. Claudio Ranieri has been installed as technical director and president of Club Italia, replacing Paolo Maldini, with a stated brief that reads like a national policy: more minutes for Italian teenagers, and, in his words, "we're too tactical abroad; we must refocus on individual technique."

And a week earlier, on 24 July, the DFB presented Jürgen Klopp as Bundestrainer in Frankfurt, on a four-year contract through Euro 2028 and the 2030 World Cup, taking office on 15 August. He replaces Julian Nagelsmann, who resigned on 3 July, four days after Germany went out on penalties to Paraguay — a third straight major tournament without a quarter-final. Klopp was released from his role as Red Bull's global head of football, brings Peter Krawietz, Pepijn Lijnders and Sven Bender with him, and framed the job in the only terms that were ever going to persuade him: "The national team can unite us Germans like hardly anything else." DFB president Bernd Neuendorf called him "our unconditional first choice."

The scheduling gods have already done their work. France and Italy have been drawn together in League A, Group 1 of the 2026-27 Nations League alongside Belgium and Türkiye. Zidane's second competitive match in charge is Italy at the Stade de France on 2 October — Zidane against Mancini, two first cycles, one of them belonging to a team that watched the World Cup on television. Klopp's Germany are in Group 2 with the Netherlands, Serbia and Greece, opening away in Amsterdam on 24 September. World champions Spain, meanwhile, have been drawn with England, Croatia and Czechia. September is going to be worth staying up for.

The British transfer record broke twice in nineteen days — and both sellers are now stuck

Elliot Anderson for £116m, then Morgan Rogers for £117m. Chelsea are the biggest spenders in world football. Nottingham Forest and Aston Villa have three weeks to find replacements that do not exist.

The record that Jude Bellingham set at £115m in 2023 stood for three years and then fell twice in under three weeks. On 2 July, Manchester City completed the signing of Elliot Anderson from Nottingham Forest for £116m on a five-year deal — a club record too, surpassing the £100m paid for Jack Grealish, and the first signing of Enzo Maresca's reign. Anderson, 23, had completed his medical in Kansas City while on England World Cup duty. He started 37 league games last season and led the entire division for ball recoveries with 306. Nineteen days later, on 21 July, Chelsea took the record off City by paying Aston Villa £117m for Morgan Rogers on a seven-year contract. "For me, Chelsea are the biggest club in London," Rogers said, which will have been read carefully in north London, where he had been the priority target of the champions.

Chelsea are not finished. Under Xabi Alonso, who took charge on 1 July, they have spent more than any club on earth this summer: Rogers at £117m, Marco Palestra from Atalanta at €57m plus €3m — the single largest fee in Italian football this window, and one that has gone almost entirely unremarked in England — Geovany Quenda from Sporting CP at £44m, Emmanuel Emegha from Strasbourg, and on Thursday Maxence Lacroix from Crystal Palace at £52m rising to £60m, on a contract to 2032. Two more are close. Fabrizio Romano declared "here we go" on Wednesday for Danny Welbeck, 35, from Brighton — terms agreed to 2028, medical pending, Brighton having approved the striker's own formal request to leave, for what is reported as a nominal fee of around £5m. And Jordan Henderson, 36, has agreed personal terms to 2028 as a free agent, with Nicolò Schira and Romano both reporting that only the termination of his Brentford contract stands in the way. Nine signings and counting, and nobody has yet published the profit-and-sustainability arithmetic.

The more interesting story is at the other end. Forest have £116m and a new manager in Oliver Glasner, and no obvious way to spend it. "It's impossible to get a player right now as good as Elliot Anderson was," Glasner admitted on 29 July, having already called the market "crazy" a week earlier; he has confirmed that blocking the sale "was never on the cards." They have signed Gustavo Sá from Famalicão for €20m and taken Xaver Schlager on a free, and their preferred replacement, Tottenham's Lucas Bergvall, is a player Forest sources themselves say "nothing is close" on. Villa's position is starker still: they have lost Rogers and Youri Tielemans (£35m to Manchester United) and Donyell Malen (£21.6m to Roma), banked more than £170m, and brought in Johan Manzambi, João Gomes and Alejandro Garnacho on loan — while opening the season against Brighton, then the champions, with a Super Cup against PSG in between. Unai Emery won the Europa League in May. He starts the defence of a Champions League place having sold his best player and his most reliable one.

Eddie Howe walks away from Newcastle — and it is the recruitment, not the results

Nearly five years, ended by mutual agreement on Thursday, twenty-five days before the season opens at home to Liverpool. Isak, Gordon and Tonali have been sold. The captain wants out. The last act was a 4–1 beating at Bristol City.

The most consequential managerial departure of the English summer was confirmed on Thursday 30 July, and it is worth being precise about why it happened, because the obvious explanation is wrong. Eddie Howe did not leave Newcastle United because he was failing. He left because he stopped believing the club could recruit well enough to keep pace with what it was selling. Sky Sports News' Mark McAdam reported that Howe himself initiated the process a number of weeks ago, with the final talks compressed into roughly forty-eight hours. The exit is by mutual agreement after nearly five years.

Read the ledger and the decision explains itself. Alexander Isak went to Liverpool for £125m on deadline day last September. Anthony Gordon went to Barcelona in May for a fee reported between £60m and £69m. Sandro Tonali went to Tottenham on 6 July for £92.5m plus £7.5m in add-ons — a Spurs club record, with Newcastle having already rejected £80m, and a £45m profit on the £55m paid to AC Milan in 2023. And captain Bruno Guimarães told the club in early July, per David Ornstein of The Athletic, that he wants to leave for a Premier League rival; L'Équipe reported on Friday that the deal is close to fully agreed at a base fee around £77m, against Newcastle's push for nearer £90m. There is a wrinkle there worth watching: Newcastle owe Lyon roughly 20 per cent of the profit, some £8m and more with add-ons, and Lyon need it. Howe's last public words as manager were about the saga: "Any distraction, if it's not healthy, is unhealthy."

Sporting director Ross Wilson and chief executive David Hopkinson are running the succession, and the expected appointment is Matthias Jaissle, currently at Al-Ahli and formerly of RB Salzburg — reported as advanced and, by TEAMtalk's Graeme Bailey, as a "done deal," though no compensation figure or contract terms have been confirmed and no club has announced anything. Treat it as agreed but not official. The incoming manager inherits a squad rebuilt around potential rather than proven quality: Bazoumana Touré from Hoffenheim at £42m, funded directly by the Tonali money, Aladji Bamba from Monaco at €41.5m on 24 July, Sean Steur from Ajax at €23m and 20-year-old goalkeeper Ewen Jaouen from Reims. He also inherits a goalkeeping problem: Newcastle chased James Trafford across three windows and have now lost him to Leeds United, who are reported to have agreed a deal with Manchester City in excess of £40m. Their fallback is Braga's Lukas Horníçek and his £25.6m release clause.

Howe's final match in charge was a 4–1 pre-season defeat at Bristol City on Wednesday 29 July. Newcastle open the season at St James' Park on Sunday 23 August against Liverpool — the club that bought Isak, now managed by Andoni Iraola, who himself replaced Arne Slot, sacked on 30 May one year after winning the title. Ten of the twenty Premier League clubs will start 2026-27 with a different manager than they finished 2025-26 with. That, more than any single fee, is the fact of the English summer.

Mourinho's Madrid go for Rodri — and Barcelona cannot get past Atlético's flat no

Real Madrid have opened formal contact for the World Cup-winning captain with fourteen months left on his contract. Barcelona are back inside LaLiga's 1:1 rule and have had €100m rejected for Julián Álvarez. Frenkie de Jong is out for months.

Spanish football's summer has a shape that would have sounded implausible a year ago: José Mourinho is the head coach of Real Madrid, appointed on 11 June on a three-year contract to 30 June 2029, after Xabi Alonso was dismissed in January following 233 days and a Supercopa final defeat to Barcelona, and after Álvaro Arbeloa's five-month interim ended on 9 June. Mourinho has not yet been formally presented; Spanish reporting suggests the unveiling has been pushed to 21 August, the eve of his competitive debut, at his own request — his Netflix documentary is released on 11 August. Make of that what you will.

The signature pursuit is Rodri. On 29 July, Fabrizio Romano reported that Madrid had contacted Manchester City about the midfielder for the first time, that Florentino Pérez had green-lit the approach after months of groundwork, and that personal terms with the player are already agreed, with City granting permission to speak to him. City's own line, also via Romano earlier in the week, was that as of Monday night no official approach had been received — a gap of a few days that tells you how fast this has moved. The valuations are apart: City want around €75m, Madrid have offered north of €50m and regard €60m as a ceiling. Rodri's contract expires in June 2027, which is Madrid's entire leverage, and City are already reported to have agreed personal terms with Lille's Ayyoub Bouaddi as a successor — a claim that Lille president Olivier Létang flatly contradicted on Friday, saying the situation "hasn't changed" and that while several elite clubs have made concrete offers, "very few clubs today can afford Ayyoub." Certainty: advanced talks, no fee agreed, and a disputed replacement chain.

Madrid have otherwise been busy at both ends. In: Marc Cucurella, Bernardo Silva, Ibrahima Konaté and Denzel Dumfries (the last from Inter for his €20m release clause on 5 July), plus 21-year-old striker Carlos Espí from Levante, confirmed official on Thursday at a reported €25m on a five-year deal to 2031. Out, and this is the story nobody is telling loudly enough, has gone the Castilla production line: Gonzalo García and César Palacios have both joined Fulham in a double deal worth around £45m, with Madrid selling 70 per cent of García's rights for a reported €40m plus variables and retaining 30 per cent and a right of first refusal on both. The decisive factor, per Ben Jacobs, was Fulham's new manager. That manager is Arbeloa, who coached both boys in Madrid's academy and was sacked by Madrid seven weeks ago. Their centre-back Víctor Valdepeñas, meanwhile, joined Fiorentina for €8m with a buy-back attached. Madrid are monetising their youth system harder than at any point in living memory, and buying the replacement in from Valencia.

Barcelona's summer has been defined by two documents. The first is financial: on 2 July the club officially re-entered LaLiga's 1:1 spending rule for the first time since 2019-20, confirmed by president Javier Tebas, after a €100m VIP-seat deal, Robert Lewandowski's departure to Chicago Fire, the €11m sale of Ansu Fati to Monaco, a renegotiated Andreas Christensen contract and record revenues at the reopened Spotify Camp Nou. The second is medical: on 23 July the club confirmed that Frenkie de Jong has torn the medial collateral ligament in his right knee, sustained at the World Cup with the Netherlands, to be treated conservatively with no return date given. Spanish reporting puts him out three to four months — most of the first half of the season. There is a genuine club-versus-country row underneath it, with claims that Dutch medical staff graded the injury as minor and cleared him to keep playing; that specific allegation is single-sourced and should be treated as unconfirmed. De Jong's own comment was pointed: "I find it difficult to see how some people question my relationship and commitment to the club over false information."

The freedom to spend has run straight into a wall named Atlético Madrid. Barcelona's declared priority is a striker to replace Lewandowski and the target is Julián Álvarez. A reported €100m bid was rejected outright. A reported €150m from Real Madrid was also rejected. Álvarez has a contract to 2030 and a €500m release clause, and Atlético's position — described in Madrid as an "intransigent stance" specifically on sales within Spain — has been a flat no. The player's own position is contested: Argentine reporting has him preferring to stay in Spain and wanting Barcelona; Spanish reporting has him publicly silent since remarks made on international duty. He is scheduled to rejoin pre-season on 10 August. Barcelona have since had 31-year-old free agent Léon Goretzka offered to them, per Matteo Moretto — Hansi Flick knows him from the German national team — with internal doubts over age and wages and no advanced negotiations. That is the tell of a club with money and no obvious way to spend it well.

Germany's teenage economy: a €30m 18-year-old, a €100m 19-year-old, and an open war over Said El Mala

Dortmund have signed Konstantinos Karetsas and picked a public fight with Köln. Leipzig cannot get Real Madrid to close on Yan Diomande. Bayern say they are done, and cannot shift €100m of unwanted midfield.

The Bundesliga's window has one theme and it is age. Borussia Dortmund have signed Konstantinos Karetsas, the 18-year-old Greece international, from KRC Genk: medical passed on Tuesday, contract signed on Thursday evening in the presence of sporting director Ole Book, with the official announcement deliberately held back until early next week so the player can say goodbye in Belgium. The fee is reported between €30m plus €2m in bonuses (kicker) and €33m plus clauses (Sacha Tavolieri), with a sell-on for Genk, who had rejected four earlier offers. He replaces Julian Brandt, who left on a free at the end of June, and arrives with 92 senior games, six goals and 23 assists behind him.

Dortmund's other pursuit has curdled into the most openly hostile negotiation in Europe. On 24 July, Sky Germany revealed a BVB bid for 1. FC Köln's Said El Mala of €26m plus €8m in "easy" bonuses and €8m in "difficult" ones — and the difficult triggers reportedly included El Mala being named Bundesliga Player of the Season, finishing as league top scorer, and being nominated for the Ballon d'Or. Köln called it a joke bid. Book responded publicly that "he is not a Borussia Dortmund player." Köln's Thomas Kessler and Tim Steidten signalled they would not entertain further proposals. By Friday, Bild reported that BVB have set an internal ceiling of around €40m while Köln demand €50m, and that a revised €34m-plus-€10m offer was also rejected. El Mala is contracted to 2030 with no release clause; he has turned down Brentford, who reportedly offered up to €50m, and Roma, because he wants to stay in Germany and specifically wants Dortmund. Köln hold every card and know it.

At RB Leipzig, the Yan Diomande saga has become an unintentional case study in how transfer certainty gets inflated. On 25 July, Real Madrid bid €90m plus €10m for the 19-year-old Ivorian winger against a Leipzig demand of €120m. On 26-27 July, Fabrizio Romano reported the transfer already complete at over €100m with a contract to 2031 and a medical booked. On 28 July, Diomande turned up for fitness testing in Leipzig and the club said many details remained open. On 30 July, Sky Germany's Florian Plettenberg had it at "close to agreement," with Madrid accelerating before Leipzig left for their training camp. It would be Leipzig's record sale, beating the €90m Joško Gvardiol fetched from Manchester City in 2023, and Oliver Mintzlaff had categorically ruled out a sale as recently as December. Five days after it was called done, it is not done. Leipzig, whose chief executive Tatjana Haenni has said plainly that "transfer income is very, very important" for the club's budget balance, are already lining up Lyon's Malick Fofana at a reported €20–30m as one of several replacements.

And Bayern Munich have declared the window shut. Two signings — Ismael Saibari from PSV for around €50m, fresh from scoring three group-stage goals for Morocco and the decisive shootout penalty against the Netherlands, and Nathaniel Brown from Eintracht Frankfurt at €50m plus €5m, their fifth-most expensive signing ever. Sporting director Max Eberl on 30 July: "I had to smile about the names being linked to us. There's 0% truth to these rumours." The reason is not thrift; it is that João Palhinha, Sacha Boey and Bryan Zaragoza cost roughly €100m between them and nobody will take them. Benfica and Aston Villa have asked about Palhinha and been told a price they will not pay. Elsewhere in Munich, Harry Kane — 61 goals and seven assists in all competitions last season, a Bundesliga goal every 78 minutes — enters the final year of his contract, with Sport Bild reporting Bayern intend to extend him through 2029 at no reduction in salary. Uli Hoeneß, on Friday: "there's really no reason for him to leave next year." And Jamal Musiala, who had the metal plate removed from his left foot in early July, returned to the training ground on Friday and is targeting the Supercup against Dortmund on 22 August.

Italy and France: Roma's €52m burst, Inter's purge, and PSG reloading around Akliouche

Gasperini's Roma spent their whole budget in two days. Inter want Cristian Romero and must sell first. And the European champions have banked €110m while agreeing €50m for Monaco's best player.

No club in Europe had a busier forty-eight hours than Roma. On Thursday they announced Santiago Castro from Bologna for €35m plus bonuses and a 10 per cent sell-on, handing the 22-year-old Argentine the No. 9 shirt, with Artem Dovbyk moving the other way on loan to June 2027 with an option. On Friday they closed Konstantinos Koulierakis from Wolfsburg — Gianluca Di Marzio reports €17m plus €1.5m in add-ons, a five-year deal, medical Saturday morning before he joins the squad in Wales. That is the €52m budget spent, and Gian Piero Gasperini still wants two more. The marquee target is RB Leipzig's Antonio Nusa, who is actively pressing Leipzig to let him go and has already spoken to Gasperini; Leipzig want €55–60m, Roma want to stay under €45m, and a summit between Gasperini, sporting director D'Amico and Ryan Friedkin was held on Friday before the squad flew out to decide whether to escalate. A €25m bid has gone in for Feyenoord right-back Givairo Read. Everything else depends on selling Matías Soulé, whom Milan want at €30–40m.

Reigning champions Inter — a 21st Scudetto under Cristian Chivu — completed the free signing of John Stones from Manchester City on Thursday, a two-year deal to 2028 at a reported €4m net per season, reuniting him with Manuel Akanji, whose €15m purchase obligation was triggered automatically by the title. "I still want to win trophies," Stones said. But the real Inter story is subtraction. Fabrizio Romano reported on Thursday that Kristjan Asllani has been formally told he is outside the project and "warmly invited" to find another club, with Davide Frattesi in a similar if less absolute position. Both must go before anything else happens, because Inter's priority target is Tottenham's Cristian Romero: Alfredo Pedullà reported on Friday afternoon that a fee of around €40m is locked with Spurs and agreement in principle reached on salary and commissions, though Fanpage's version has the player still asking €8m net. Certainty: fee agreed, wage talks live, contingent on sales.

At Juventus, where Giovanni Carnevali has replaced Damien Comolli as chief executive, the return of Randal Kolo Muani from PSG was declared done on 29 July: a loan with an obligation to buy triggered by qualification for any European competition — a significant negotiating win, since PSG's Luis Campos had demanded the obligation be Champions League-only — in a package worth up to €50m, with the player on €5.5m a season over five years. Except that on Friday Carnevali said only: "difficult that he arrives today." It is not signed. Juventus have also agreed a framework with Bayer Leverkusen for Kerim Alajbegović at €30m plus €5m and a resale percentage, beating Milan to him, and are hunting a goalkeeper, with Tottenham's Guglielmo Vicario the primary target and Parma's Zion Suzuki the alternative — though PSG are also well placed on Suzuki, whom Parma value at a minimum €30m. They begin the season having lost Dušan Vlahović on a free transfer when his contract expired, which is the kind of thing that gets executives replaced.

In France, the European champions are reloading rather than rebuilding. PSG have banked roughly €110m by selling Gonçalo Ramos to Milan for €70m including bonuses and Kang-in Lee to Atlético Madrid for €40m on 25 July, and have agreed a €50m fee with Monaco for Maghnes Akliouche, who has given his own agreement after meeting Luis Enrique. Monaco had rejected €45m before accepting. It is not yet officially announced — Foot Mercato still had it "in progress" on Friday — so call it fee agreed and player agreed, awaiting paperwork. They have also signed Lucas Digne from Aston Villa via a release clause, twenty-three days before PSG meet Villa in the Super Cup, and have agreement with the player on Ajax winger Mika Godts, with the clubs still apart. The unresolved question is Bradley Barcola: Liverpool want him, Romano reported on 28 July that the clubs were set to talk, and the valuations range absurdly from Liverpool's reported £75m to PSG asks quoted between £102m and £145m across different outlets. Barcola has declined a PSG extension and the club's own project is his priority. Akliouche's arrival is widely understood to be the thing that unlocks him.

The Rumour Mill

The live sagas still simmering, with the certainty stated plainly. Where a story rests on one outlet, we say so.

Where is Mohamed Salah? This is the strangest loose end of the summer. Salah left Liverpool as a free agent on the expiry of his contract — that much is confirmed — and as of Friday, no club has announced him. His agent has teased an announcement "very soon." The Saudi Pro League is the strongest link and MLS has reportedly been ruled out, but there is no reporting solid enough to name a destination. Certainty: departure official, destination unknown.

Rafael Leão to Turkey. Fenerbahçe executives visited Casa Milan on 28 July. Milan have dropped their ask from €70m to €50m; Turkish bids sit at €40–45m; on wages, Fenerbahçe are offering €7–8m against Leão's demand of €12m-plus, against his current €6.5m. Galatasaray are also in. Sky Italia reported the player had accepted a move; Pianeta Milan reported on Friday that the deal may collapse entirely, with Fenerbahçe pivoting to Crystal Palace's Ismaïla Sarr. Corriere dello Sport expects no resolution before late August. Certainty: advanced interest, no formal offer, three outlets and three different readings of the player's willingness.

A £22.5m goalkeeper for a promoted club. Fabrizio Romano reported on 29 July that Carl Rushworth is joining newly promoted Coventry City from Brighton in a package worth £30m — £22.5m up front plus add-ons and a sell-on — with the medical booked. Set that beside Leeds' reported £40m-plus for James Trafford and the goalkeeper market has quietly reset. Frank Lampard's Coventry, Championship winners, open the season away at the champions on Friday 21 August. Certainty: fee agreed, medical booked, not announced.

Vinícius Júnior's contract. The renewal talks at Real Madrid are at an impasse, and Marca reported on Friday that Madrid would be open to selling if no agreement is reached. Other Madrid outlets insist he stays. There is no bid. Certainty: stand-off, single-sourced on the willingness to sell, exit unlikely — but this is the renewal story of the Spanish window.

Marseille's defence is being picked apart. Foot Mercato's Santi Aouna reported on 30 July that Leonardo Balerdi is leaving with multiple Serie A offers, that Bayer Leverkusen are intensifying talks for Facundo Medina — whom Marseille bought from Lens for €18m five weeks ago — and that Nayef Aguerd had a €15m release clause valid only until 31 July, with Rennes strongly interested. That clause expired on Friday; whether Rennes triggered it is the single most time-sensitive open question in Ligue 1. Meanwhile Manchester City have bid for goalkeeper Gerónimo Rulli, who is willing to back up Gianluigi Donnarumma, with Toulouse's Guillaume Restes lined up as the replacement. Certainty: advanced talks across the board.

Lens are about to break their transfer record — on the way out. Two of the best-sourced reporters in the market converged this week: Santi Aouna had agreement reached on 28 July for Mamadou Sangaré to Brentford at €48m plus bonuses; Fabrizio Romano put it at £41m on Friday. The Coupe de France holders and Champions League qualifiers have already lined up the replacement: Yacine Titraoui from Sporting Charleroi has completed his medical. Certainty: fee agreed on both, neither announced.

Also simmering. Trevoh Chalobah to Como has been €5m apart for a month (Chelsea want €30m plus €5m; Como offer €25m plus €5m). Miguel Gutiérrez to Bayer Leverkusen is at an identical €5m impasse with Napoli, per Florian Plettenberg and Bild. Liam Delap has three Premier League suitors with Everton reportedly leading. Alex Scott saw a £64m Chelsea bid rejected by Bournemouth. Manu Koné is wanted by Manchester United at a reported £42.5m. And Lewis Ferguson has a €20m-plus offer from Real Betis that Bologna have met with what Italian media call "a wall" at €40m, with Atalanta and Roma watching. One to disregard: Romano explicitly denied on 29 July that Mauro Icardi, a free agent since leaving Galatasaray, is a Como target, despite a rash of headlines saying otherwise.

AROUND THE LEAGUES

Premier League

The season begins on Friday 21 August, a week later than usual — the league cites 89 clear days from the end of last season and 33 from the World Cup final, explicitly on player-welfare grounds — and ends on Sunday 30 May 2027. It opens with the champions at home to Coventry City under the lights, and the standout tie of the opening round is Hull City hosting Manchester United at lunchtime on the Saturday, Hull's first top-flight fixture in a decade. Newcastle host Liverpool on the Sunday, and Fulham host Chelsea on the Monday night. The Community Shield is on Sunday 16 August; the window closes at 23:00 BST on Tuesday 1 September.

The defining structural fact is the turnover in the dugouts: half the division has changed manager. Pep Guardiola left Manchester City at the end of last season despite a contract to 2027, replaced on 29 June by Enzo Maresca on a three-year deal, with City paying Chelsea compensation "well in excess of £10m." Arne Slot was dismissed by Liverpool on 30 May, a year after winning the title and a season after finishing fifth on 60 points; sporting director Richard Hughes interviewed exactly one candidate, Andoni Iraola, who signed a two-year deal. Xabi Alonso is at Chelsea. Roberto De Zerbi is at Tottenham on a five-year contract with Alessandro Nesta as his assistant, rebuilding from 17th with roughly £237m spent and "seven or eight" signings planned. Oliver Glasner swapped Crystal Palace for Nottingham Forest; Pierre Sage took Palace; Marco Rose took Bournemouth; Gary O'Neil took Ipswich Town after Kieran McKenna's exit; and Álvaro Arbeloa, sacked by Real Madrid in June, is at Fulham — the last club in the league to fill its vacancy, with one signing made, and Chelsea at home on the opening Monday.

One story deserves more attention than it is getting. De Zerbi, speaking on Friday, described how Tottenham signed Sandro Tonali: he contacted the midfielder before the 2025-26 season had ended, through a mutual friend in Brescia, sending word that "if we stay up, I go to him" — a message despatched before Spurs' decisive fixture against Chelsea. No club has complained and no regulator has acted, but it is a candid public account of approaching a contracted player, and Newcastle, who eventually sold him for £100m, may take a dimmer view now it has been said out loud. Elsewhere: Crystal Palace, Conference League winners in May, have lost both first-choice centre-backs inside seven months, Marc Guéhi to Manchester City in January and Lacroix to Chelsea on Thursday, and are replacing them with Augsburg's Chrislain Matsima — a deal Sky Germany reported at over €30m with personal terms agreed, but which German sources said on 30 July had been called off by Augsburg — and a 19-year-old from Brest with six senior appearances. Sunderland, seventh and into the Europa League in their first season back, have made exactly one signing, a free transfer, while selling their best young forward. And the injury picture across the division after a full World Cup remains almost entirely unpublished, which is itself remarkable three weeks out.

La Liga

Spain is first out of the blocks: the season begins on the weekend of 14–16 August, a full week before England and two before Germany, with the first Clásico at the Spotify Camp Nou on Sunday 25 October. Barcelona are the reigning champions and open at home to Athletic Club. There is a live question over whether Real Madrid's opener against Real Sociedad has been moved to 26 August with a trip to Espanyol on the 22nd instead, on account of their World Cup contingent; that is single-sourced and unresolved. Note the promotion and relegation: Real Oviedo, Girona and Mallorca all went down, with Racing Santander back after fourteen years, plus Deportivo La Coruña and Málaga. Seven clubs changed manager. Diego Simeone begins his sixteenth season at Atlético, which is now roughly three times the tenure of anyone else in the division, and has spent €102m net on Alejandro Grimaldo, Morten Hjulmand and Kang-in Lee while refusing to sell Álvarez and saying goodbye to Antoine Griezmann, whose contract was terminated in March so he could join Orlando City. Edin Terzić is the new man at Athletic, where Nico Williams is tied to a contract reported to run to 2035 and has repeated that he would sign for life if it were up to him — though renewed English interest is circling his release clause, and his chronic pubalgia, which limited him to 32 appearances last season before he played a World Cup, is worth watching.

Bundesliga

Germany starts last, on Friday 28 August, with Bayern Munich hosting VfB Stuttgart — a rematch of a Pokal final Bayern won 3–0 in Berlin in May, with a Harry Kane hat-trick, to complete the Double. The DFB-Pokal first round runs 21–24 August with Bayern's and Dortmund's ties pushed into early September, and the Franz-Beckenbauer-Supercup is on Saturday 22 August at the Signal-Iduna-Park, Borussia Dortmund against Bayern, free-to-air on Sat.1. The composition of the division has changed sharply: Schalke 04 are back after three years, SC Paderborn after six, and SV Elversberg arrive as the 59th club in Bundesliga history — while VfL Wolfsburg went down for the first time since their 1997 promotion, losing the relegation play-off to Paderborn. The fallout there is the most under-covered financial story in Germany: Wolfsburg's budget has been cut from €80m to €55m, automatic relegation clauses have reduced player wages by 35 per cent, they are carrying 38 players against an ideal of 25, and Volkswagen chief executive Oliver Blume announced in March that the company would reassess its engagement with the club entirely. In a league built on 50+1, a carmaker publicly reviewing whether it still wants to own a football club is not a small thing. Schalke, meanwhile, are in advanced talks for Napoli's Jesper Lindstrøm at the personal request of coach Miron Muslic.

Serie A

The season opens on Saturday 22 August, with Inter hosting Monza and Torino against Milan the pick of the round; the first Derby della Madonnina falls on matchday 10. Nine of twenty benches changed hands, and the carousel is worth memorising: Massimiliano Allegri has replaced Antonio Conte at Napoli, Rúben Amorim is at Milan to 2029, Luciano Spalletti has a full season at Juventus, Maurizio Sarri is at Atalanta with a strict one-out-one-in policy and a potential €150m sales pot, Gennaro Gattuso has landed at Lazio four months after losing the national team, and Ivan Jurić is at Monza. Two numbers frame the league's position. The first is that Atalanta's €60m sale of Marco Palestra to Chelsea is the largest fee Italian football has produced this window and was barely reported in England. The second is the television money: Serie A distributed €887m net for 2025-26, with Inter taking over €80m at the top and four clubs under €30m at the bottom — a top-to-bottom ratio of 3.3 to 1, against the Premier League's 1.6 to 1, and on a pot roughly a quarter the size. That, not coaching, is why Sandro Tonali said this week that he and his family "had decided to return to Italy, but it was impossible because of money." Elsewhere: Alessandro Buongiorno had meniscus surgery on 20 July and will miss two to three months of Napoli's season, and the agreement between the city of Milan and the Lombardy region on the new San Siro was signed this summer, with authorisations targeted by summer 2027, construction to begin in the second half of that year, and the whole thing explicitly tied to Milan's candidacy to host the closing stages of Euro 2032.

Ligue 1

France returns on the weekend of 22–23 August with a genuinely unusual innovation: eighteen clubs and no midweek matchdays at all, ratified by the LFP board, with the last round of 2026 on 12–13 December and a resumption on 2–3 January. PSG won a fourteenth title and a fifth in a row, clinched with a game to spare on 13 May, and then retained the Champions League, beating the English champions on penalties in Budapest after Ousmane Dembélé's second-half equaliser — only the second club to retain the trophy in the Champions League era. Lens won the Coupe de France and host PSG in the Trophée des Champions at the Stade Bollaert-Delelis on Sunday 16 August. Underneath the glamour, the numbers are alarming. The LFP projects €412.2m in total television rights for 2026-27, of which — after operating costs, the CVC repayment, Ligue 2 funding and parachute payments — only around €184.1m is actually distributable to the eighteen top-flight clubs, or between €3.6m and €11.7m each. The league's own streaming platform, Ligue 1+, sits at roughly one million subscribers against a business-plan target of 1.5 million, with a €63.5m reserve fund plugging the hole. French clubs are now structurally dependent on player trading to balance their books, which is precisely what you are watching when Lens sell Sangaré for €48m and replace him with a €1.2m signing from the Belgian league, or when Lyon — whose parent Eagle Football Group posted a €201.2m net loss and €517.9m of debt, and who were provisionally relegated by the DNCG in June 2025 before winning on appeal — entertain selling Malick Fofana to Leipzig.

Major League Soccer

MLS is the only one of the six actually playing, having restarted on the weekend of 16–19 July after a six-week World Cup shutdown, and it is playing in front of the biggest names it has ever assembled. This is the last spring-autumn season in league history: MLS switches to a summer-spring calendar from 2027-28, bridged by a shortened fourteen-match "Sprint Season" in early 2027. The secondary transfer window opened on 13 July and, for the first time in two decades, runs into September to align with Europe — and clubs have used it. Casemiro joined Inter Miami as a free agent on 22 July, a Designated Player alongside Lionel Messi and Germán Berterame, on a contract through the 2027 Sprint Season with an option to June 2029, arriving directly from Brazil's World Cup. Antoine Griezmann scored on his debut for Orlando City in a 4–0 win at San Jose, creating five chances, and then won his home debut 1–0 against the league leaders. Robert Lewandowski is at Chicago Fire. Son Heung-min is in the form of his MLS life at LAFC, and Thomas Müller is at Vancouver. One Orlando supporter's framing of it is the thesis of the whole era: "it's about players coming and wanting to experience America," rather than retiring there.

On the pitch, Nashville SC lead the Supporters' Shield race and Inter Miami are the form team with six straight wins, most recently a 3–2 defeat of Chicago in which Luis Suárez scored twice and 20-year-old Preston Plambeck got the winner — with Messi and Rodrigo De Paul both rested after Argentina's run to the final. The Western Conference is a three-way tie on 33 points between Vancouver, LAFC and San Jose. LA Galaxy, eleventh in the West and absent from Leagues Cup after missing last year's playoffs, are the season's outstanding disappointment. Leagues Cup begins on Tuesday 4 August and, for the first time, includes matches played in Mexico — 36 clubs, every group-stage fixture MLS against Liga MX, with Seattle Sounders defending the title they won by beating Inter Miami 3–0 in the 2025 final. And in Charlotte on Wednesday the MLS All-Stars beat the Liga MX All-Stars 4–3, making it 4-1 all-time. On the national team, Mauricio Pochettino is reported by David Ornstein to be close to a four-year extension through 2030 after the United States won their group and lost 4–1 to Belgium in the round of 16. Not official yet.

IN BRIEF

FIFA opens a case against Argentina. On 29 July, FIFA began formal disciplinary proceedings against the AFA, four players and an assistant coach over conduct at the World Cup, including the post-final altercation and a banner reading "Las Malvinas son Argentinas" displayed after the semi-final. Nahuel Molina, Leandro Paredes and assistant Roberto Ayala face potential assault charges; Thiago Almada and Spain's Gavi face unsporting-behaviour charges. No bans have been issued and a decision will follow "in due course."

A record changed hands twice in four days. Lionel Messi became the World Cup's all-time leading scorer during the tournament, and then Kylian Mbappé took it off him in the third-place play-off, finishing on 22 goals in 22 matches to Messi's 21 in 33.

The Finalissima will not be replayed. UEFA has confirmed the 2026 edition between Spain and Argentina is cancelled outright, not postponed, after the parties could not agree a date or venue. CONMEBOL's president had declared Argentina the winners by default; Spain then beat them in the World Cup final.

Aston Villa carry a UEFA sanction into the Champions League. Reporting on UEFA's 30 June financial-sustainability decisions has Villa facing a €22.5m fine, entirely conditional on improving their squad-cost ratio, plus a restriction on Champions League List A registrations — a third consecutive season under scrutiny. Juventus, Newcastle, Chelsea and Nottingham Forest also feature. These figures come from a single independent analyst and have not been verified against UEFA's own published case list.

The internationals who stopped. Confirmed retirements from national-team football since the tournament: Cristiano Ronaldo (Portugal), Neymar (Brazil), Manuel Neuer (Germany), Guillermo Ochoa (Mexico, after a sixth World Cup), Sadio Mané (Senegal), Riyad Mahrez (Algeria), Marko Arnautović (Austria) and Patrik Schick (Czechia). Messi has not announced anything; Argentine reporting suggests a farewell match is being planned, but no AFA official has said so on the record.

A Cinderella qualifier and a Danish miracle. In the Champions League second qualifying round, Kauno Žalgiris secured Lithuania's first-ever league-phase place, and AGF Aarhus overturned a 4–1 deficit against Lech Poznań and won on penalties, reaching this stage for the first time since 1987-88. In the Conference League, Austria Wien were eliminated by Latvia's Liepāja.

Thomas Tuchel's future is still unresolved. The England manager was publicly non-committal after the semi-final exit and was booed during the third-place win. The FA has not announced a decision. It is the biggest open managerial question left in the game.

FIFA changed the rules mid-tournament. Mandatory three-minute hydration breaks were introduced at the 22nd and 67th minutes of every match regardless of local temperature, added to stoppage time. Gianni Infantino pre-emptively insisted there was "no additional revenue for FIFA" in them. Goals per game rose to 2.91, from 2.69 in 2022.

Serie A now has thirteen foreign-owned clubs after a US fund led by Brett Johnson — the group that bought Ipswich Town in 2021 — took majority control of newly promoted Frosinone on 1 July. Fourteen is widely regarded as the tipping point for league governance. Italian outlets disagree on the buyer's name, so treat the identity as provisional.

Coming back from a four-year ban. Hamburger SV are preparing for the return of defender Mario Vuškovič, whose four-year doping suspension has expired — comfortably the longest road back in German football.

Two more goodbyes. James Milner retired at Brighton, Ashley Young at Ipswich, Niklas Süle at Dortmund, Nicolas Höfler at Freiburg, Florian Kainz at Köln and Dante at Nice, at 42.
