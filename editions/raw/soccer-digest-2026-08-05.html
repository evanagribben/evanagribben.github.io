The Lead

He Tried to Sell a Fifth of the World Cup. Now Football's Most Powerful Man Is Fighting for His Job.

 

Ten days ago Gianni Infantino was unopposed for re-election. Today he sits in a hastily convened crisis meeting in Rabat, three of FIFA's six confederations in open revolt, his own senior staff resigning around him, and UEFA formally declaring that it no longer has confidence in his leadership. This is how the ground gave way — and what happens next.

The World Cup that finished in New Jersey on July 19 was, by almost every commercial measure FIFA cares about, the most successful sporting event ever staged: 104 matches, three host nations, roughly 6.6 million spectators, and a governing body sitting on a revenue cycle worth some $15 billion. Ten days after the confetti was swept out of MetLife Stadium, Gianni Infantino attempted to convert that success into something permanent, private and — critically — partly his own. It has come closer to ending his presidency than anything in his decade in charge.

The vehicle was to be called FIFA Forward Enterprise. According to reporting first published by The Times on July 29 and carried widely thereafter, FFE would have been a subsidiary holding the commercial rights — broadcast, sponsorship, ticketing — to the men's and women's World Cups and the Club World Cup. It was valued at roughly $20 billion. FIFA would have retained majority ownership; the 211 member associations would have taken stakes; and private investors, reportedly including Joshua Kushner's Thrive Capital, would have been invited to put in something in the order of $8.2 billion. Infantino himself, per the same reporting, would have become "commissioner" of the new company once his FIFA presidency ends in 2031, on a salary benchmarked against the NFL's.

It is not the first time this idea has been floated inside FIFA House — a $25 billion structure involving SoftBank and Saudi money died in 2018 — but it is the first time a president has attached a deadline to it. Member associations were reportedly given until September 19 to approve, with the strong implication that development funding would follow the votes. That detail, more than the money, is what detonated the thing.

On July 30, UEFA announced it would boycott FIFA competitions. Its language was not the language of negotiation. The plan was "irresponsible and indefensible" and had been "conceived in secret"; once private investors hold ownership, UEFA argued, "every decision shaping the future of football is no longer driven by what best serves the game." It demanded the proposal be abandoned "in its entirety," with binding assurances against any future private ownership. CONCACAF held an emergency meeting the same day and rejected the plan on process grounds without going as far as a boycott. The AFC also opposed it, in a line that will follow Infantino around for a while: "meaningful democracy is not measured solely by the opportunity to vote."

"None of us are the owners of football. It is not FIFA's to sell."

 

UEFA, August 1

Do the arithmetic and the scale of the problem becomes obvious. UEFA, the AFC and CONCACAF between them control 136 of FIFA's 211 votes. Infantino did not lose a fringe of dissenters; he lost the room.

The internal collapse was worse. Carlos Cordeiro, Infantino's senior adviser and a former president of U.S. Soccer, resigned in protest: "I cannot stand by while FIFA considers selling a stake in the World Cup." Kevin Lamour, FIFA's chief operating officer, publicly opposed the project and said staff had been misled about it. Secretary General Mattias Grafström described "a sad and reproachable series of events" and promised employees would be shielded from the politics. Arsène Wenger, FIFA's chief of global football development, called the eventual withdrawal "absolutely necessary and beyond question" while conspicuously placing himself outside the project. When the people running your organisation start briefing that they were kept in the dark, the argument is already over.

Infantino abandoned the plan on July 31. His statement — "it has become clear that the project has created divisions… no longer in the interest of the objective set out" — was an attempt to draw a line. It did not work. On August 1, UEFA escalated, declaring that it had lost confidence in his leadership and calling for "a full and robust review of FIFA's leadership and governance." The English FA said it "fully supports" that position. CONCACAF endorsed it. Morocco's and Qatar's federations, pointedly, backed Infantino. And Prince Ali Bin Al Hussein of the Jordan FA went further than anyone, alleging that FIFA had conditioned assistance on financial matters upon his endorsement of the president: "the whole situation amounts to blackmail and we refuse to give in to that."

Which brings us to today. Infantino has summoned senior FIFA staff to Rabat, Morocco, for a crisis meeting on Wednesday. Sky Sports' Kaveh Solhekol, one of the few reporters to have covered every twist of it, has described the president's position as looking untenable and has raised openly the possibility that he resigns at the meeting rather than be forced out over the coming months. As this edition went out — noon Pacific, mid-evening in Morocco — no outcome had been reported. Treat any claim to the contrary with suspicion until it carries a name you recognise.

The succession chatter is already loud, which is itself a tell. Sepp Blatter, of all people, has publicly backed Lise Klaveness, president of the Norwegian federation, arguing that "at FIFA, the time is right for a woman to take the lead." Victor Montagliani, the CONCACAF president, is reported to be weighing a challenge. Nasser Al-Khelaïfi — the Paris Saint-Germain president and ECA chairman, whose club has just retained the Champions League — is the name European officials keep floating in the background. Candidates have until November 2026 to declare for the March 2027 election. Until a fortnight ago, Infantino was unopposed and had the declared backing of roughly 200 of the 211 associations. England and Wales have since rescinded theirs. U.S. Soccer chief executive JT Batson said on August 4 that the federation "stands with Concacaf and its members" but has committed to no candidate, and will decide at its September board meeting.

There is a temptation to file this under boardroom noise. Resist it. The specific proposal is dead, but the pressure that produced it is not: a governing body with a four-year revenue cycle, a 48-team tournament to service, an expanded Club World Cup to sell, and an investor class that has spent five years buying its way into every other sport on earth. Somebody will try this again. The interesting question raised by the last ten days is not whether Infantino survives to March — it is whether the confederations have just discovered that, acting together, they can say no. That is a structural change, and it will outlast whoever is sitting in the chair in Zurich.

The Secondary Feature

 

Newcastle Sold the Project, Then the Manager Walked. Now the Captain Is Going Too.

 

£240m of outgoings, a head coach gone 25 days before the opener, and a 38-year-old from the Saudi Pro League handed the keys. The most alarming summer in the Premier League belongs to a club that finished in Europe.

Start with the sequence, because the sequence is the story. On July 29, Newcastle United lost 4-1 at Ashton Gate to Championship side Bristol City. On July 30, Eddie Howe left the club after nearly five years. Sky Sports reported the decision had been "in the pipeline for two weeks" and "had nothing to do with the match" — which is true, and also beside the point. Howe's objections were structural: Sandro Tonali sold to Tottenham for £100m, Anthony Gordon sold to Barcelona for £69m, and his captain being courted from London. Jamie Carragher's verdict on Sky was blunt: "it just looks like he hasn't really got the energy for it anymore."

Two days later Newcastle appointed Matthias Jaissle, 38, on a four-year deal, paying Al Ahli £9.5m in compensation. It is a genuinely interesting appointment — back-to-back AFC Champions League titles in Saudi Arabia, two Austrian titles and a first Champions League knockout stage at RB Salzburg — and a wildly exposed one. Kieran McKenna and Antonio Conte were also considered. Jaissle's first match in charge is a friendly at Valencia on Saturday. His first Premier League fixture is Liverpool at St James' Park.

And then, on Tuesday, the last domino. Sky Sports News reported that a £75m fee had been agreed for Bruno Guimarães and that the captain had been granted permission to travel for a medical. Sky's own framing is that Newcastle "didn't want to sell" and "were desperate to keep" him; they relented only when the player made clear he saw this as his last chance to win titles, and only at a number reported to be among the highest ever paid for a player over 28. He arrived from Lyon in 2022 for £40m and leaves with 195 appearances and 31 goals. An earlier bid, in June, had been rejected outright. The reversal took barely a week after Howe's departure — which tells you either that the player's mind was the only variable, or that it wasn't.

The counter-argument is that Newcastle have not been idle. Five signings have arrived for a projected £140m-plus: Bazoumana Touré from Hoffenheim (€50m), Aladji Bamba from Monaco (€36m plus €5m in add-ons, a five-year deal), Sean Steur from Ajax, Ewen Jaouen from Reims, and — officially on August 3 — the Czech goalkeeper Lukáš Horníček from Braga for £25.7m on a five-year contract, his release clause met. Sporting director Ross Wilson called his recent progress "remarkable." That is a coherent, young, resale-friendly squad plan. It is also a plan built by selling three of the four players anyone outside Tyneside could name.

There is a regulatory shadow over all of it. In June, UEFA fined Newcastle €3m plus a further €3m, with €7m suspended, for breaching both the squad-cost rule and the football earnings rule — one of four Premier League clubs sanctioned. From this season the Premier League has retired PSR and replaced it with a Squad Cost Ratio capping on-pitch spend at 85% of football revenue, with clubs in UEFA competition held to UEFA's stricter 70%. Read the summer through that lens and the sales look less like a loss of nerve and more like arithmetic.

The one line Newcastle have held is Lewis Hall. Manchester United prepared a £60m opening bid; TEAMtalk's Graeme Bailey reported on August 4 that Newcastle have told United he is not for sale and that it would take "in excess of £62m." David Ornstein went further on August 5: "I do NOT expect Lewis Hall to be coming to Man United. Following the departure of Eddie Howe, Newcastle will be MORE determined to keep him, maybe even tie him down to a new contract." For a club that has spent the summer saying yes, that is the first meaningful no — and it may be the most important thing they do before September 1.

Spain

 

Real Madrid Agree a Club-Record €125m for Diomande — and Nobody at Leipzig Is Confirming Anything

 

Bought from Leganés for €20m twelve months ago. Sold, if the paperwork lands, for up to €140m. Meanwhile the club's biggest star is a year from free agency and €8m apart from a renewal.

Sky reported on Wednesday that Real Madrid and RB Leipzig have reached agreement on Yan Diomande: €125m guaranteed, plus up to €15m in bonuses of which around €10m is considered realistic. CD Leganés, who sold him to Leipzig for €20m last summer, hold 5% of Leipzig's profit — roughly €5.25m. If the add-ons land, the 19-year-old Ivorian becomes the most expensive signing in Real Madrid's history, past Jude Bellingham's €127m in 2023. It would also be, by a distance, Leipzig's record sale.

The certainty tier matters here, because Leipzig have been unusually pointed about it. Sporting director Marcel Schäfer said on the record on August 4: "A few days ago, one or two so-called transfer experts were saying the deal was done, or posting 'here we go' — but that's simply not the case. We're not there yet." Leipzig confirmed on Wednesday that Diomande had recovered from an illness and rejoined training at their Saalfelden camp. Read the two together and you get the shape of it: an agreed structure, an unsigned contract, a medical outstanding, and a selling club that would like the world to stop announcing it. Fee reporting also diverges — ran, citing Sky and Marca, has carried "approximately €130m plus bonuses."

Diomande earned this in a single season: 12 goals and eight assists in 33 Bundesliga appearances, and the league's Rookie of the Season award. José Mourinho, appointed on June 11 on a three-year deal after Madrid paid €15m to release him from Benfica, is understood to see him mainly on the right, with cover on the left. Which brings us to the reason the deal is being pushed through in the first week of August.

Vinícius Júnior's contract expires in June 2027. He earns €17-17.5m a year. Madrid have offered €22m and have briefed, repeatedly, that the number is final and immovable because of the wage hierarchy; his camp wants around €30m including bonuses. Talks are being run by chief scout Juni Calafat, and a decisive meeting was set for this week. Late on Tuesday, BBC, ESPN, The Athletic and Marca all reported Madrid had improved the offer and were awaiting an answer. From January he can negotiate freely with foreign clubs. Madrid's reported asking price if they sell is around €150m — and the only Premier League club treated as a serious suitor is one whose business this edition does not cover.

Elsewhere in Mourinho's rebuild, the free transfers are quietly the best business anyone has done anywhere: Ibrahima Konaté from Liverpool and Bernardo Silva from Manchester City, both for nothing, plus Marc Cucurella from Chelsea (~€55m) and Denzel Dumfries from Inter (€20m release clause). Silva trained at Valdebebas for the first time on August 4: "It was impossible to say no when Real Madrid came calling. I did not think twice." The one that has stalled is Rodri. The World Cup Golden Ball winner has made it plain the move is Madrid or nothing, but the clubs are roughly €15m apart — City want €75m, Madrid have been working at around €60m — and Madrid's internal hesitancy is medical as much as financial: since his 2024 knee injury he has missed a materially higher share of available days, and he is recovering from back surgery that will keep him out of the Community Shield. City, for their part, want to open renewal talks instead.

England  ·  France

 

Salah Is Gone to Trabzonspor. Liverpool's Answer Is a €150m Standoff With the European Champions.

 

Nine years, 257 goals, two titles and a European Cup — ended on a free transfer and a broken relationship. Now Andoni Iraola needs a right winger, and PSG know it.

It became official on Wednesday: Mohamed Salah has signed for Trabzonspor on a free transfer, a two-year deal running to the summer of 2028. He left Liverpool in May as a free agent, having announced his departure in March, and departs Anfield with 257 goals in 442 appearances, two Premier League titles and a Champions League. The ending was not clean. Despite 29 goals and 18 assists in the title season, his minutes collapsed under Arne Slot; he was dropped for three consecutive matches in late December, said publicly that he had "no relationship with the coach," and accused Slot of throwing him under the bus. As recently as July 31 he was reported to be weighing the Saudi Pro League, Beşiktaş and a two-year Designated Player offer from Sporting Kansas City. Trabzonspor won the race in under a week.

Liverpool's replacement plan runs through Paris, and it is not going smoothly. Bradley Barcola has, on the reporting of L'Équipe relayed by Media365 on August 5, already agreed personal terms; the obstacle is entirely club-to-club. Paris Saint-Germain want €150m excluding bonuses. Liverpool's opening offer was north of €100m, with reporting placing their ceiling at €120m. Fabrizio Romano cautioned on August 2 that "this is not going to be something imminent" and warned the thing could become a weeks-long saga; Barcola, who has refused to extend a deal running to 2028, does not return to training until early September, which gives PSG time and Liverpool a headache. One quirk worth noting: PSG and Liverpool share a minority shareholder in Arctos, and Nasser Al-Khelaïfi is expected to speak to Liverpool's ownership directly.

Liverpool have opened a second front on the same club. RMC Sport reported on August 4, via Foot Mercato's Maxime Barbaud, that they have accelerated for 18-year-old Ibrahim Mbaye, with PSG expected to want €40-50m; Aston Villa, Tottenham and Manchester City are also in. Mbaye made 42 appearances last season but fell out of favour with Luis Enrique over training intensity. Liverpool's confirmed business so far is Jeremy Jacquet from Rennes (£55-60m, figures vary by outlet) and Víctor Muñoz from Osasuna (€40m); their pre-season has been mixed, including a 4-2 collapse against Leeds in Chicago after leading 2-0, and Joe Gomez is out for around a month with a muscle injury, ruling him out of the opener at Newcastle.

Italy

 

One Rule Has Split Serie A Into Two Leagues. Como Spent €127m. Lazio Signed Two Free Agents.

 

The costo del lavoro allargato threshold dropped from 80% to 70% in June. This is the first window under it, and it explains almost everything strange about the Italian summer.

Italy's expanded labour-cost index measures amortisations, salaries and contributions against revenue. Since June the ceiling has been 70%, down from 80%, and the sanction for non-compliance is a transfer freeze of the kind Lazio suffered a year ago. Six clubs were flagged: Lazio, Napoli, Atalanta, Torino, Genoa and Fiorentina. Look at what each has done since and the rule writes the transfer news for you.

Lazio, running at roughly 81% against a 70% requirement, have signed exactly two players — Alfonso Pedraza and Danilho Doekhi, both free — while selling Mario Gila to Milan for €30m and Ivan Provedel to Inter. Gennaro Gattuso, appointed in June, asked for four or five signings to build "a more offensive team, with a high defensive line and rapid transitions." He has two free agents. Napoli, sitting almost exactly on the threshold, are in what Corriere Azzurro calls "profound and worrying stasis": Aurelio De Laurentiis has mandated sell-before-buy, 47 contracted players need placing, and their only arrivals are three loan redemptions — Rasmus Højlund made permanent at €50m, Vanja Milinković-Savić at €21m, Alisson Santos at €20m. On Wednesday they finally banked something, selling Miguel Gutiérrez to Bayer Leverkusen for €30m on a deal to 2031 — the biggest sale abroad by an Italian club this window. Massimiliano Allegri, appointed on July 3 on a contract to 2029, inherits the gridlock.

Now the other league. Como, Champions League qualifiers for the first time in their history, have committed roughly €127m — Italian commentators have noted, not entirely admiringly, that they "couldn't care less about financial fair play." Nico Paz is back on loan from Real Madrid, Álvaro Morata has arrived from Milan, Trevoh Chalobah is through his medical at €30m plus €3m on a contract to 2031 — Fabrizio Romano called that one done on August 3 — and Yan Couto is essentially agreed from Borussia Dortmund at a €2m loan plus a ~€20m option. Cesc Fàbregas is now chasing Moise Kean from Fiorentina at a €40-45m asking price, with Joshua Zirkzee the alternative. Juventus have spent about €150m gross — Randal Kolo Muani made permanent at €38m plus €12m on August 1, Loïs Openda at €45m, Kerim Alajbegović at around €37m — and Fiorentina have made eight signings, the eighth being Franco Mastantuono, who agreed personal terms on Wednesday for a dry loan from Real Madrid with no purchase option, made possible only because Madrid are subsidising his wages.

And then there is the champions' answer, which is no answer at all. Inter won their 21st Scudetto with 87 points and have run a window at exactly zero net spend — €34m out, €34m in, per Tuttosport. Giuseppe Marotta: "We can make irrational operations, but that's not our modus operandi." On August 4, Corriere della Sera supplied the likely reason: Oaktree are starting the process of finding buyers for the club, two years after taking it from Suning over unpaid loans. No valuation, no named bidders, no urgency — but clean books before a sale would explain a great deal. Cristian Chivu has taken John Stones and Manuel Akanji from Manchester City, the former for nothing, and is chasing Nico González from Juventus to replace Dumfries. His problem on Wednesday was a fresh one: Football Italia reported that Atlético Madrid can match Tottenham's €40m valuation of Cristian Romero, a defender Inter had hoped to sign cheaply.

Spain  ·  The Governance Story

 

Atlético Have Reported Barcelona to the Federation and the Agent to FIFA. The Álvarez Saga Has Left the Pitch.

 

A €100m bid rejected, a public transfer request, a CEO telling Joan Laporta to "stop insisting" — and a disciplinary case that could, in theory, cost points. August 10 is the date to circle.

On July 31 the RFEF opened a formal disciplinary case against Barcelona, following a complaint lodged by Atlético Madrid on June 30 alleging an improper approach to Julián Álvarez, who is under contract at the Metropolitano until 2030. Atlético's filing alleges "a sustained campaign of pressure and harassment." Reported potential sanctions run from fines and partial stadium closure to a points deduction and a transfer ban — though it should be said clearly that this is press characterisation of the maximum available range, not an RFEF statement of intent. Barcelona's defence is that Álvarez's own World Cup comments — that a transfer "represented the best decision for his future" — pre-dated any formal approach. Separately, Atlético have filed a FIFA complaint against the agent, Fernando Hidalgo.

The temperature was set on July 23, when Atlético CEO Miguel Ángel Gil Marín said in a YouTube interview that the club would reject €100m, €150m or €200m, told Joan Laporta to "stop insisting," and accused him of keeping the saga alive for publicity. He attacked Hidalgo's counsel while explicitly exempting the player. Barcelona's €100m offer has been rejected; direct club-to-club communication has broken down entirely. On August 5, Football España reported that sporting director Deco met Hidalgo discreetly in Madrid to keep the thing alive. Paris Saint-Germain remain in ongoing discussions. Álvarez, who made a public transfer request six weeks ago and believes Atlético's leadership broke a promise to let him go, has said he will not breach his contract or rebel publicly against Diego Simeone. He reports for pre-season on August 10.

What gets lost in the noise is Atlético's actual problem, which is that they may be about to start a season without a centre-forward. Antoine Griezmann left on a free for Orlando City after 211 goals and 97 assists in 488 appearances. Alexander Sørloth is expected to go for around €30m. Álvarez wants out. That is a hole of roughly 93 goals from the last two seasons, to be filled by Ademola Lookman as a false nine — he scored twice in a 4-1 friendly win over Getafe on July 30 — and two academy forwards. Simeone has spent much of the summer in New York; sporting director Mateu Alemany is reported to be operating without full authority; and multiple executives keep issuing competing public statements. The signings have been serious — Morten Hjulmand at €40m plus €5m, Kang-in Lee from PSG on a six-year deal, Álex Grimaldo from Leverkusen — but none of them scores goals. Their opener is at home to Málaga on August 19.

England  ·  France

 

Manchester City Have Agreed Terms With Rodri's Successor. Lille Would Quite Like Him for One More Year.

 

Ayyoub Bouaddi is 18, made his name at the World Cup, and is valued by his club at nine figures. The sticking point isn't the fee. It's the calendar.

Santi Aouna broke it for Foot Mercato on July 30: Manchester City and Ayyoub Bouaddi have an agreement, and the obstacle between the clubs is timing rather than money. "Lille souhaite conserver Ayyoub Bouaddi une saison supplémentaire sous la forme d'un prêt" — Lille want to keep him another season on loan; City want him integrated now. By August 3-4, Florian Plettenberg was reporting a full verbal agreement on personal terms, understood to be a six-year deal, corroborated by Get French Football News. Lille president Olivier Létang has valued him at around €100m, explicitly benchmarking against the €135m City paid Nottingham Forest for Elliot Anderson, and said publicly that he thinks the player "should stay with us for another season." Létang made what French media called a "grosse mise au point" on the case on July 31.

The strategic logic is transparent: City see the Morocco international as Rodri's succession plan, which is precisely why Rodri's own move to Madrid has become live at the same moment. Lille are already lining up replacements — Marca, relayed by Foot Mercato on August 5, links them to Jorge Cestero Sancho, a 20-year-old defensive midfielder at Real Madrid Castilla, possibly on loan. City's window has otherwise been about the record: Elliot Anderson at £116m is both a club record, beating Grealish's £100m, and the most expensive English player ever. Enzo Maresca, announced on June 29 as Pep Guardiola's successor, has also lost Bernardo Silva and John Stones on frees and James Trafford to Leeds for £40m plus £5m — a Leeds club record and a world record for an English goalkeeper. The 115 charges, for the avoidance of doubt, remain undecided; the commission concluded its hearing in December 2024 and has not reported. Anything you read claiming a verdict this week is recycled.

Around the Leagues

Premier League

 

Season opens Friday, August 21. Eight new managers — a record. And the rulebook has been rewritten twice.

Eight of the twenty clubs will start the season with a new manager, which is a Premier League record, and the list reads like a small European congress. Xabi Alonso is at Chelsea. Enzo Maresca has succeeded Pep Guardiola at Manchester City. Andoni Iraola has left Bournemouth for Liverpool and been replaced by Marco Rose. Roberto De Zerbi is at Tottenham, Oliver Glasner has moved from Crystal Palace to Nottingham Forest, Pierre Sage has gone the other way into Selhurst Park, Álvaro Arbeloa — sacked by Real Madrid in June — has landed at Fulham, Gary O'Neil is at Ipswich, and Matthias Jaissle arrived at Newcastle on Saturday. Michael Carrick and David Moyes keep their jobs; Keith Andrews has signed a contract at Brentford running to 2032, which is either visionary or a typo.

Tottenham are the window's runaway spenders, at a reported £240m and climbing: Sandro Tonali for a club-record £100m, Mateus Fernandes for £85m, Jan Paul van Hecke for £52m from Brighton, plus Andy Robertson, Marcos Senesi and Martin Dúbravka on frees. Chelsea have broken their own record for Morgan Rogers at £117m and added Maxence Lacroix, Geovany Quenda on a deal to 2034, Marco Palestra, Emmanuel Emegha, Valentin Barco, and — in the transaction most likely to make you check the date — Danny Welbeck and Jordan Henderson on frees. Manchester United's summer has been more modest: Andrey Santos from Chelsea at around £48m, Youri Tielemans via a £35m release clause, and Rasmus Højlund and Casemiro off the wage bill. Altay Bayındır was at contract stage on Wednesday for a loan to Celta Vigo with a €4m option, per Romano. Two clubs stand out for restraint: Everton, with a net spend of about minus £34.5m and no incoming fees at all, and Brentford, minus £56.6m with zero transfer income — a striking break from the model that made them.

Two regulatory shifts are worth understanding, because they will define the next three years. PSR is gone. In its place sit the Squad Cost Ratio — on-pitch spend capped at 85% of football revenue, 70% for clubs in Europe, with fines above the line and points deductions above a 115% "Red Threshold" — and Sustainability & Systemic Resilience, a three-part test on working capital, liquidity and positive equity. Clubs voted down an accompanying proposal that could have brought in a salary cap. In June, UEFA fined four English clubs: Aston Villa €22.5m with a registration restriction for the Champions League campaign, Newcastle €3m plus €3m, Chelsea €3m, Forest €2.5m. Villa's subsequent sales of Morgan Rogers and Youri Tielemans — roughly £152m out — are the visible consequence. Separately, in March, Chelsea took the largest fine in Premier League history, £10.75m, plus a nine-month academy transfer ban, for financial reporting breaches in the Abramovich era; the league confirmed no PSR breach would have occurred had the payments been disclosed. On the pitch: Coventry City are back in the top flight after 25 years under Frank Lampard, alongside Ipswich and Hull City, who signed Konstantinos Tzolakis from Olympiakos on Wednesday for around £20m — their biggest signing ever, with eleven more expected. And a friendly result you will want to see written down: Bournemouth 10, Genoa 1, on August 4, across four 30-minute periods, which Genoa gamely described as "a conjoined training session."

La Liga

 

The season opens on Saturday, August 15, with a matchday split across nearly two weeks — Real Madrid do not play until August 26, Barcelona not until the 27th, at home to Athletic Club. Barcelona are the defending champions, a third title in a row, and enter their first full season back at a completed Camp Nou. Their window has been shaped by one number: Anthony Gordon at €70m plus €10m in variables, which El Desmarque reported carries an estimated €24m in agent commissions on top, taking the true cost above €104m — agent fees at roughly a third of the transfer value, which specialists describe as abnormally high. Karim Adeyemi arrived from Dortmund at €22m. Robert Lewandowski left on a free for Chicago Fire, ending Barcelona's first season since 2021-22 without him. João Cancelo has agreed a pay cut for a permanent return from Al-Hilal at up to €10m, reported by RAC1 and COPE, though it was still being described as difficult to complete on Wednesday. And the real emergency is in midfield: Frenkie de Jong tore his MCL at the World Cup, per Matteo Moretto, and his relationship with Hansi Flick is reported to have deteriorated badly. Javi Guerra of Valencia, at around €40m, is the live target.

Elsewhere: Athletic Club, under Edin Terzić, have signed nobody at all and need to cut payroll before they can. Villarreal released Dani Parejo and Thomas Partey and took Péter Gulácsi from Leipzig on a free after eleven years and 296 Bundesliga appearances. Sevilla are selling again, with Akor Adams gone to Venezia for €16m and César Azpilicueta retired. Celta Vigo have been the busiest club in the division this week, agreeing loans for Bayındır, Hugo Cuenca and Abdoulaye Faye. Of the promoted three, Deportivo La Coruña have been much the most aggressive — Pierre-Emerick Aubameyang from Marseille for €1.5m, Leo Román for €9m, and Angeliño on loan from Roma, official Wednesday — while Racing Santander, up after 14 years, have brought Sergio Canales home from Monterrey on a free. Girona went down. Liga F returns in August under a new title sponsor with Barcelona defending.

Bundesliga

 

Germany starts last, on Friday, August 28, with Bayern Munich — 89 points, 122 league goals, a 34th title and a Double — at home to VfB Stuttgart. Before that comes the Franz Beckenbauer Supercup at Signal Iduna Park on August 22, Borussia Dortmund against Bayern. The biggest German story of the summer was not a transfer: on July 24 the DFB appointed Jürgen Klopp as head coach on a four-year contract effective August 15, covering Euro 2028 and the 2030 World Cup, after Julian Nagelsmann resigned following Germany's shootout elimination by Paraguay. President Bernd Neuendorf called Klopp "unsere uneingeschränkte Wunschlösung." He brings Peter Krawietz, Pepijn Lijnders and Sven Bender. Bayern's own news is nearly as significant: on the Asia tour, Manuel Neuer confirmed this will be his last season as a professional, with Sport Bild reporting a plan for Jonas Urbig to take significantly more minutes now and the No. 1 shirt from 2027-28.

Dortmund's window turns on Konstantinos Karetsas, official on August 4 at €30m plus €2m from Genk — a Genk record — and the detail is lovely: Genk wanted €35m, Dortmund refused, and the 18-year-old Greece international pushed his own club to accept the lower figure. Lars Ricken: "Kostas had a not insignificant part in that with his attitude." He then turned up at a gyros restaurant in Kamen-Methler within hours of signing to meet supporters. Sporting director Ole Book confirmed on Wednesday they still want pace and one-v-one dribbling after selling Karim Adeyemi. Bayer Leverkusen have executed the most radical teardown in the league, losing Piero Hincapié, Álex Grimaldo and — in the trade of the summer — Kerim Alajbegović, bought from Salzburg for €8m and flipped to Juventus for €32m inside the same window; Miguel Gutiérrez arrived Wednesday from Napoli as Grimaldo's replacement on a deal to 2031. SC Freiburg have quietly run the best business in Germany, banking €60m for Johan Manzambi and €25m for Merlin Röhl, and drew HJK Helsinki or Motherwell in the Conference League play-off. Schalke 04 are back after three years with Edin Džeko, 40, signed permanently on August 4 having reportedly taken a substantial pay cut — "I really only wanted to keep playing for Schalke" — but carrying roughly €96.6m of negative equity, and the league's equity rule requires a 10% annual reduction or an automatic three-point deduction. They are exempt this season as a promoted club. Next season they will have to sell. Wolfsburg went down for the first time since 1997; SV Elversberg come up as the 59th club in Bundesliga history.

Serie A

 

The Italian week belonged to a funeral. Franco Baresi died aged 66, having been under treatment since pulmonary nodules were found in August 2025, and was buried on August 4 at the Basilica di Sant'Ambrogio in Milan, the service televised on Rete 4. Thousands lined the streets. Paolo Maldini, Marco van Basten, Daniele Massaro and Dejan Savićević were among the mourners. A banner read: "Legend, immortal, inimitable flag that will never stop waving. Ciao Captain." It was also, unavoidably, a political occasion: AC Milan owner Gerry Cardinale was loudly jeered arriving and leaving, with the familiar chants demanding he sell, and was drowned out when he tried to speak to reporters afterwards. President Paolo Scaroni and Mayor Giuseppe Sala also drew audible disapproval. On Wednesday, Luka Modrić — who has extended at Milan and intends to play until he is 41 — explained his decision partly in the language of Baresi's legacy: "I still have the passion."

Nine of the twenty benches changed. Rúben Amorim is at Milan on a deal to 2029 and is cutting the squad to around 25 for a 3-4-2-1, with Adrien Rabiot as his leadership cornerstone and Gonçalo Ramos — €65m plus €5m, the most expensive signing in the club's history — as his centre-forward. Maurizio Sarri is at Atalanta with Cristiano Giuntoli alongside him as sporting director, and their friendlies have not been reassuring. Gennaro Gattuso is at Lazio, four months after leaving the Italy job. Massimiliano Allegri is at Napoli. Roberto Mancini, meanwhile, was announced on July 28 as Italy's new head coach — his second spell — with Claudio Ranieri moving in as technical director and, as of Wednesday, Gianfranco Zola appointed youth co-ordinator. Italy failed to qualify for a third consecutive World Cup, confirmed on March 31; Gabriele Gravina resigned as FIGC president in April; Andrea Pirlo withdrew from consideration over a betting-site scandal; and Paolo Maldini and Leonardo resigned seventeen days after being appointed. The federation has been through five months of institutional demolition. In brighter news, Roma Women completed a domestic double, a third Scudetto in four years plus the Coppa Italia, and Fiorentina will retire Giancarlo Antognoni's No. 10 for their centenary season. Serie A opens August 23, with Inter at home to Monza.

Ligue 1

 

The season opens on the weekend of August 22-23, six days after Lens host Paris Saint-Germain in the Trophée des Champions at Bollaert — the first time in years the curtain-raiser has been played in France, and at the cup winners' own ground. Lens won their first Coupe de France last season, finished second in the league, and have used the €48m club-record sale of Mamadou Sangaré to Brentford to fund a rebuild under Dino Toppmöller, including Yacine Titraoui from Charleroi on August 3 — their third Belgian-league signing of the window, nicknamed the "Algerian Verratti," who chose Lens over Olympiakos specifically to play in the Champions League.

Two clubs are in genuine difficulty. Olympique Lyonnais lost 2-1 at Sparta Prague in the Champions League third qualifying round on Tuesday, on their return to the competition after six years. They led through an own goal on the stroke of half-time, Corentin Tolisso blasted a 66th-minute penalty over the bar, and Matěj Ryneš equalised and then set up John Mercado's winner. Paulo Fonseca afterwards: "Nous n'avons pas fait ce que nous avions préparé." The second leg is at the Groupama Stadium next Tuesday, and defeat drops them into the Europa League. Meanwhile Olympique de Marseille have completed a total front-office regime change — Pablo Longoria and Medhi Benatia both gone, Stéphane Richard installed as president, Grégory Lorenzi as sporting director, Bruno Genesio poached from Lille as head coach — and have, as of August 5, signed nobody at all. Lorenzi's stated plan is to balance the sporting project against "economic constraints" and to be "more inventive in recruitment." Aguerd is leaving; Bilal Nadir and Pierre-Emerick Aubameyang already have. They have at least beaten Al-Shahania 3-0 on Wednesday for a third straight friendly win, through Igor Paixão, Himad Abdelli and Quinten Timber.

PSG, meanwhile, are champions of Europe for a second consecutive year, having beaten Arsenal 4-3 on penalties in Budapest on May 30 after a 1-1 draw — only the second club in the Champions League era to retain it. They play six competitions this season, including the UEFA Super Cup and the FIFA Intercontinental Cup, and their pre-season is compressed to almost nothing: their first friendly of the summer was on Wednesday, away at Mallorca. They have made exactly one confirmed senior signing, third-choice goalkeeper Alessandro Longoni, while fielding bids for Barcola and Mbaye and chasing Godts, Ferran Torres and Suzuki. Elsewhere, Strasbourg signed Giovanni Reyna from Borussia Mönchengladbach on August 4 for a reported €3m on a five-year deal, ending seven years of German football for the 23-year-old American; Rennes took Charlie Cresswell from Toulouse; and Nice, now under Olivier Pantaloni, announced Gauthier Hein. Troyes and Le Mans are up, the latter after sixteen years; Metz and Nantes went down.

Major League Soccer

 

MLS has a new commissioner, only its third. On August 3 the Board of Governors elected Larry Berg, co-managing owner of LAFC, to succeed Don Garber after 27 years. Garber remains commissioner through 2026 and becomes chairman in 2027; Berg takes office January 1, 2027, and must divest his LAFC stake. He is a 30-year Apollo Global Management veteran who also holds interests in AS Roma and Swansea City, and in his first remarks he immediately teased changes to roster rules and competition format. His in-tray is genuinely heavy: launching the new summer-spring calendar for 2027-28, negotiating a new CBA, renegotiating media rights — the Apple deal runs to 2028-29 at roughly $250m a year, with some owners targeting $400-500m — and resolving venue issues including Vancouver's expiring BC Place lease. On the same weekend, U.S. Soccer announced that Mauricio Pochettino has extended as USMNT head coach through the 2030 World Cup, with an expanded remit across youth development and coaching education, and the 2028 Copa América in between. His World Cup: a group won with a record six points, a first knockout win in 24 years, then a 4-1 defeat to Belgium in the round of 16, with Folarin Balogun the team's top scorer on three.

The league is paused. After a single round on July 31-August 1, MLS does not return until August 15, with the Leagues Cup occupying the fortnight in between. It began on August 4 and runs to the final on September 6 — 36 clubs, all 18 Liga MX sides plus the 18 MLS clubs that made the 2025 playoffs, all Phase One matches cross-league, no draws (ties go straight to penalties, worth two points to the winner), and for the first time ever, matches staged in Mexico. Opening night was not kind to MLS: three regulation wins, one shootout loss and two defeats, including two genuine upsets. Atlante, on their tournament debut, won 1-0 at BC Place against Western Conference leaders Vancouver through Jhojan Julio, and FC Juárez won 2-1 at Allianz Field. Tigres beat Real Salt Lake 6-5 on penalties after Zach Booth missed. The MLS wins came from FC Cincinnati 3-1 over Pachuca — Evander has now scored in three consecutive Leagues Cup openers — Columbus 3-1 over Atlas, and Charlotte 3-0 over Pumas after an 82-minute weather delay.

The Supporters' Shield race is led by Nashville SC on 40 points from 18, unbeaten but once at home, with Inter Miami two behind and unbeaten in seven. Lionel Messi — contracted through 2028 — has 12 goals and eight assists in 14 games and came off the bench in a 2-2 draw with Columbus on August 2 alongside a returning Rodrigo De Paul; Luis Suárez has seven goals in his last four league matches but misses the entire Leagues Cup, serving a six-match ban from the 2025 final. In the West, Vancouver and LAFC are level on 34, Vancouver with two games in hand. Son Heung-min leads the league in assists and was MVP of the All-Star Game on July 29, scoring twice as MLS beat Liga MX 4-3 in Charlotte. At the other end, Atlanta United have 12 points, Sporting Kansas City 14 with the worst goal difference in the league, and Philadelphia Union, the reigning Shield holders, sit 27th on 16 — a collapse that deserves its own investigation. Big names continue to arrive: Robert Lewandowski at Chicago Fire, Antoine Griezmann at Orlando City, Casemiro and De Paul at Miami, Brais Méndez at Columbus, Thomas Müller at Vancouver, Kyōgo Furuhashi at the LA Galaxy. In the NWSL, the league has terminated its Victory+ media deal over unfulfilled payments and moved matches to free streaming on NWSL+, and Tobin Heath will retire via a one-day contract with the Portland Thorns on August 22.

In Brief

Oaktree are quietly shopping Inter. Corriere della Sera reported on August 4 that the fund has begun looking for buyers, two years after taking the club from Suning. No valuation, no bidders named, no urgency stated — but it retroactively explains a champion running a zero-net-spend window.

Naples want to rebuild the Maradona; De Laurentiis wants to leave it. Mayor Gaetano Manfredi presented a €200m reconstruction on August 4, taking capacity from 54,732 to 63,300, removing the athletics track and targeting completion by 2030 with a Euro 2032 bid in mind. Napoli's president has not responded, and continues to push for a club-owned ground outside the city.

The UEFA Super Cup is PSG against Aston Villa — August 12 in Salzburg, with no extra time; level after 90 goes straight to penalties. Villa qualify as Europa League winners and are chasing a first Super Cup since 1982. Johan Manzambi, their €60m arrival from Freiburg, will miss it with a knee injury.

The Premier League has rewritten some laws. From this season, injured players must leave the pitch for at least a minute; throw-ins and goal-kicks carry a five-second countdown with possession forfeited for exceeding it; substitutes cannot enter until a minute of play has elapsed; and VAR will now review second-yellow red cards.

Barcelona's Alexia Putellas has joined London City Lionesses on a free. A two-time Ballon d'Or winner heading to a newly promoted WSL club is the standout move of a busy women's window that also took Katie McCabe and Beth Mead out of north London and Georgia Stanway back into it. The WSL season starts September 4.

Crvena Zvezda won a Champions League qualifier 9-0 on aggregate, against Larne, and Sturm Graz beat Hearts 6-0 across two legs. Lech Poznań and Shamrock Rovers went out; Kairat Almaty and Aarhus survived shootouts. In Tuesday's third-round first legs, Union Saint-Gilloise and Bodø/Glimt drew 3-3 and Olympiakos were held 0-0 at home by NEC Nijmegen.

Leeds paid a world record for an English goalkeeper. James Trafford arrives from Manchester City for £40m plus £5m in add-ons, a club record beating Rutter's, on reported wages of £130,000 a week. FIFA's post-World Cup rest rules meant he was unavailable until August 8.

Stuttgart's Ermedin Demirović has ended the speculation himself. "Leaving was never an option for me," he said on August 4, after nine goals in 19 Bundesliga games from January and a World Cup run to the last 32 with Bosnia brought European suitors calling.

Leipzig are trying to shed seven players. Sporting director Marcel Schäfer took 35 to their Austrian camp and wants 28: "Too much dissatisfaction in the dressing room and on the pitch — we don't want that." Named candidates include Lutsharel Geertruida, Eljif Elmas, Arthur Vermeeren, Conrad Harder and Lukas Klostermann.

A Note on Sourcing

Every transfer above is labelled at the stage it had actually reached at noon Pacific on August 5, not the stage a headline claimed.
